Economic Complexity Reconsidered

By Mohammed I. Almajed · English · 30 min read

A diagnostic reading of the gap between export structure and productive capability. The paper examines what complexity indicators reveal, what they leave unseen, and why strategic judgment requires evidence from firms, learning and industrial ecosystems.

Economic Complexity Reconsidered Why Export Structure Is Not a Proxy for Productive Capability — and the Strategic Risks of Treating It as One

A Diagnostic White Paper Problem Definition and Interpretive Design Brief

Prepared as part of the Istawra trilogy on capability, structure, and sovereign industrial execution

Overall Description

Over the past decade, economic complexity frameworks have become central to how countries reason about diversification, structural transformation, and future growth paths. Originally developed as descriptive tools to interpret observed trade patterns, these frameworks are now widely operationalized in national strategies, sector prioritization exercises, and investment narratives.

This paper argues that a critical interpretive shift has occurred: economic complexity metrics are increasingly treated as proxies for underlying productive capability rather than as downstream descriptions of observed outcomes. This conflation introduces structural decision risk. By optimizing for export appearance, classifications, and rankings, countries risk misreading the depth, resilience, and maturity of their actual industrial capabilities.

Economic Complexity Reconsidered does not critique the mathematics of complexity metrics, nor does it propose alternative models, sectors, or policy prescriptions. Its purpose is diagnostic. The paper redraws the epistemic boundary between productive capability and its observable expressions, clarifying what economic complexity can legitimately signal—and where its use becomes strategically unsafe.

Positioned downstream of education and human capital formation, and upstream of industrial execution, this paper functions as a corrective lens for senior decision-makers. Its objective is to reduce misinterpretation risk before strategic commitments, capital allocation, and execution bets are made on the basis of structural signals that may not reflect underlying reality.

Chapter 1

The Rise of Economic Complexity as a Decision Instrument

Economic complexity frameworks were not conceived as tools for strategy. They emerged as analytical devices designed to describe patterns in international trade—specifically, how the diversity and sophistication of a country’s export basket correlate with income levels and long-run development trajectories. In their original formulation, these frameworks occupied a clearly bounded role: they offered a way to observe structure, not to infer capability, and to describe outcomes, not to prescribe action.

Over time, this boundary eroded.

As complexity measures gained visibility, they migrated from academic analysis into policy discourse. What began as a means of characterizing export structure was gradually adopted as a language for economic potential. Complexity scores, product spaces, and adjacency maps started to appear in national development strategies, diversification plans, and investment narratives. In this transition, economic complexity ceased to function solely as a descriptive lens and became, instead, a decision-bearing instrument.

This shift did not occur through explicit choice or formal endorsement. It emerged through accumulation. Each successive use of complexity metrics in strategic contexts reinforced the impression that they reflected something fundamental about a country’s productive capacity. Over time, interpretation hardened into assumption. What was once read as an observed pattern came to be treated as an underlying condition.

The appeal of economic complexity in this role is understandable. It offers an apparently rigorous, data-driven way to reason about economic structure. It translates the messy realities of production into legible visualizations, rankings, and trajectories. It promises coherence in a domain that is otherwise fragmented across firms, sectors, and technologies. For decision-makers under pressure to demonstrate progress toward diversification, complexity metrics provide a compelling narrative of movement and momentum.

Yet it is precisely this interpretive convenience that introduces risk.

By design, economic complexity frameworks operate downstream of production. They capture what is already being exported, classified, and recorded. They do not observe how goods are produced, how capabilities are accumulated, or how resilient those capabilities are under competitive stress. When these metrics are elevated from description to diagnosis, they begin to stand in for phenomena they do not directly measure.

The result is a subtle but consequential inversion. Instead of productive capability explaining observed complexity, observed complexity is taken as evidence of capability. This inversion rarely appears explicit in strategic documents, but it is implicit in how complexity metrics are used to justify sectoral bets, sequence industrial moves, or signal readiness for advanced activity.

As economic complexity became embedded in strategic workflows, its outputs acquired performative power. Rankings shaped perception. Adjacency maps framed opportunity sets. Export classifications became shorthand for industrial maturity. In this environment, complexity ceased to be a passive descriptor and became an active reference point around which expectations, capital, and institutional attention were organized.

The risk is not that economic complexity frameworks are incorrect, but that they are being asked to carry interpretive weight beyond their epistemic limits. When descriptive tools are operationalized without re-examining their boundaries, they can quietly reshape decision logic. Over time, this reshaping affects not only what is seen as possible, but what is deemed prudent, ambitious, or premature.

This chapter establishes the starting point for reconsideration. Before evaluating what economic complexity can and cannot tell us about productive capability, it is necessary to recognize how and why it came to be treated as a strategic instrument in the first place. Only by understanding this evolution can the subsequent chapters clarify where interpretation has drifted—and why that drift matters.

Chapter 2

What Economic Complexity Actually Measures—and What It Does Not

Economic complexity frameworks rest on a deceptively simple observation: countries tend to export certain products together, and the pattern of these co-exports contains information about the structure of production. From this observation, complexity metrics infer relative positions within a global trade network. The resulting measures describe how diversified an export basket is and how rare or ubiquitous its constituent products appear across countries.

At their core, these metrics measure observed trade outcomes. They operate on recorded exports, classified by product codes and aggregated at the national level. The underlying data reflects what crosses borders, how it is categorized, and how frequently similar baskets appear elsewhere. This is the object of measurement, and it is important to state it plainly.

What economic complexity does well is characterize structure as expressed through trade. It captures patterns of specialization, highlights correlations between export diversity and income, and reveals how certain products tend to cluster in global production networks. Used within this scope, complexity offers a compact way to describe where an economy sits relative to others in the space of traded goods.

What it does not measure is productive capability itself.

Productive capability resides in places the data does not reach. It is embedded in firms, workers, routines, tools, supplier relationships, and accumulated learning. It reflects not only the ability to produce a given good, but to do so reliably, at scale, under cost pressure, and in the face of technological change. These attributes are largely invisible to trade statistics.

Economic complexity does not observe how much of a product’s value is generated domestically versus imported. It does not distinguish between deep design competence and shallow assembly. It does not capture how production responds to shocks, whether firms can adapt processes, or whether an industry can sustain improvement over time. All of these dimensions are central to capability, yet they sit outside the metric’s field of vision.

The distinction between measurement and inference becomes critical at this point. Complexity metrics measure export structure. Capability is inferred. The inference may be plausible in some contexts, especially when supported by long histories of industrial learning. But it is not guaranteed, and it is not uniform across countries, sectors, or stages of development.

The danger arises when this inferential step is taken as automatic. When export sophistication is read as proof of underlying know-how, the metric begins to function as a proxy it was never designed to be. The uncertainty in the inference is flattened, and the conditions under which the relationship holds are no longer examined.

This flattening is reinforced by aggregation. National-level measures obscure firm-level heterogeneity. A small number of capable firms can produce exports that suggest broad competence where little exists. Conversely, substantial domestic capability may remain invisible if it does not yet translate into export volume. Complexity metrics cannot discriminate between these cases.

None of this renders economic complexity useless. It clarifies its proper domain. The metrics are effective at describing what is visible in trade, not at revealing what is embedded in production. Confusing these domains is not a technical error; it is an interpretive one.

This chapter draws a firm boundary. Economic complexity measures outcomes, not capabilities. Where that boundary is respected, the framework remains informative. Where it is crossed, the metric begins to speak on behalf of realities it does not observe, and strategic conclusions are drawn on unstable ground.

The following chapter turns to what lies beyond that boundary: the nature of productive capability itself, and why it resists easy observation through structural proxies.

Chapter 3

Capability, Know-How, and the Invisible Foundations of Production

Productive capability is not a static attribute, nor is it a single skill that can be acquired and then possessed. It is a layered, cumulative phenomenon that emerges over time through repeated production, error correction, and learning under constraint. Unlike outputs, which can be counted and classified, capability is embedded in processes and relationships that are rarely visible in aggregate data.

At the core of productive capability lies know-how. This know-how is not limited to formal knowledge or codified instruction. It includes tacit understanding of materials, tolerances, sequencing, failure modes, and trade-offs that only become apparent through sustained practice. Much of this knowledge cannot be easily transferred or documented; it is learned by doing and retained through continuity of work.

Capability also resides in production routines. These routines govern how tasks are coordinated within firms, how quality is maintained, how defects are identified, and how improvements are integrated. They reflect organizational memory and discipline rather than isolated technical competence. Two firms may produce the same product for export, yet differ radically in their ability to scale, adapt, or recover from disruption.

Equally important is the ecosystem in which production occurs. Supplier networks, tooling providers, maintenance capabilities, testing facilities, and logistics systems all contribute to industrial depth. Capability is distributed across these interfaces. When any one layer is thin or dependent, the apparent strength of the whole can be misleading.

Learning curves play a central role in this dynamic. Early production success does not imply maturity. Many capabilities exhibit long periods of fragility before processes stabilize and costs decline. During this phase, output may exist, and exports may occur, but the underlying system remains vulnerable. The difference between a nascent capability and a robust one is not visible in product codes or export volumes; it is revealed only under pressure.

These characteristics make capability difficult to observe directly. They also explain why it is often inferred indirectly through outcomes such as exports. The inference is understandable, but it is incomplete. Outputs provide signals, not guarantees. They indicate that production has occurred, not that it can be sustained, extended, or replicated across contexts.

This gap between appearance and substance is particularly pronounced in environments where production is fragmented, heavily integrated into global value chains, or supported by external inputs. In such cases, exports may reflect access and coordination rather than deep domestic mastery. Without careful interpretation, the presence of an output can be mistaken for the presence of capability.

The invisibility of capability creates a temptation to rely on proxies. Economic complexity metrics offer one such proxy, translating observed outputs into structured narratives about economic potential. Yet proxies are only as reliable as the assumptions that link them to what they stand in for. When those assumptions are not examined, the proxy can displace the underlying reality in decision-making.

This chapter underscores a simple but consequential point: productive capability is built, not observed. It accumulates through learning, coordination, and repetition, and it reveals itself most clearly when tested. Any framework that claims to represent capability without engaging these foundations must be treated with caution.

The next chapter examines what happens when this caution is absent—when outputs are allowed to masquerade as capabilities, and structural signals begin to substitute for industrial depth.

Chapter 4

When Outputs Masquerade as Capabilities

The misinterpretation of economic complexity becomes most consequential when outputs are treated as evidence of underlying capability. At this point, descriptive signals begin to substitute for productive reality, and the distinction between what is observed and what is assumed collapses. This collapse does not usually occur through explicit error, but through gradual normalization of inference.

Exports are particularly susceptible to this misreading. Once a product appears in a country’s export basket—especially if it is classified as sophisticated or rare—it is often taken as proof that the country possesses the corresponding industrial capability. The presence of the output is assumed to confirm the presence of the process that generated it. In practice, this assumption is frequently unwarranted.

Outputs can be generated under a wide range of conditions that do not imply deep capability. Production may rely on imported machinery, foreign technical teams, turnkey facilities, or narrowly scoped assembly operations. Firms may execute within tight tolerances for a specific product while lacking the broader engineering, design, or process knowledge required to evolve beyond it. In such cases, the output exists, but the capability is shallow, fragile, or externally anchored.

Economic complexity metrics do not discriminate between these conditions. They register the product and its co-occurrence with others in global trade data, not the nature of the production system behind it. When complexity scores rise as a result, the increase can be misread as a strengthening of industrial foundations rather than as a change in observed structure.

This misreading is reinforced by aggregation and abstraction. At the national level, a small number of firms can produce outputs that signal competence across an entire sector. At the level of product codes, differences between design-led manufacturing and contract-based production disappear. The metric smooths over heterogeneity and presents a unified picture that is easier to interpret than it is to interrogate.

Once outputs are accepted as stand-ins for capabilities, strategic decisions follow. Sectors are labeled “present.” Adjacencies are deemed “reachable.” Scaling is treated as a matter of investment rather than of learning. The implicit question shifts from “can this be built?” to “how fast can this be expanded?”—a shift that assumes away the most difficult part of industrial development.

The consequences of this substitution often emerge later, during execution. Projects stall when local suppliers cannot meet specifications. Costs remain stubbornly high despite apparent sophistication. Adaptation proves difficult when market conditions change. These outcomes are frequently attributed to implementation failure rather than to misdiagnosis, allowing the underlying interpretive error to persist.

Importantly, the problem is not confined to any single sector or stage of development. The temptation to equate outputs with capabilities appears wherever pressure exists to demonstrate progress quickly. Complexity metrics, by providing structured and comparative signals, can unintentionally legitimize this shortcut.

This chapter highlights a critical failure mode: when outputs masquerade as capabilities, decision-makers inherit confidence without control. The system appears more advanced than it is, and strategic commitments are made on the basis of signals that do not reflect the true state of industrial readiness.

The next chapter examines how this dynamic feeds into a broader pattern—optimization for appearance rather than substance—and why such optimization can be rational in the short term while destructive over the long term.

Chapter 5

The Appearance Trap: Optimizing for Structure Rather Than Substance

Once economic complexity signals acquire strategic significance, they begin to shape behavior. What starts as interpretation gradually becomes incentive. When rankings, classifications, and adjacency maps are treated as indicators of progress, actors respond by optimizing toward what is measured rather than toward what is difficult to observe. This is the appearance trap.

In this trap, structural indicators become targets. Export diversification, product presence, and complexity scores are pursued not as by-products of capability accumulation, but as objectives in their own right. The distinction between demonstrating structure and building substance blurs, and effort is redirected toward outcomes that register quickly in the data.

The trap is reinforced by institutional dynamics. Complexity metrics are legible, comparable, and easily communicated. They travel well across reports, presentations, and dashboards. In contrast, capability-building is slow, uneven, and resistant to simple representation. When performance is assessed through visible structure, it is rational for institutions to prioritize what can be shown over what must be endured.

This dynamic does not require bad faith. It arises naturally in environments where progress must be signaled to multiple stakeholders—political leadership, investors, partners, or the public. Complexity indicators offer a way to demonstrate movement without waiting for the long maturation cycles of industrial learning. Over time, however, the reliance on such indicators reshapes strategic focus.

The appearance trap also distorts sequencing. Activities that produce visible outputs early are favored over those that strengthen foundations but yield no immediate signal. Learning-intensive phases are compressed or bypassed. The system appears to advance, yet its underlying capacity to absorb complexity, adapt processes, or recover from shocks remains underdeveloped.

Importantly, optimizing for appearance can coexist with genuine effort. Facilities are built, firms are established, and exports occur. The problem is not inactivity, but misalignment. Resources flow toward achieving structural markers rather than toward deepening the capabilities that would make those markers meaningful. When pressure mounts, the system lacks the resilience that the indicators implied.

Economic complexity frameworks, when misused, can unintentionally legitimize this pattern. By presenting structure as a coherent and progressive space, they suggest that moving through that space is equivalent to advancing in capability. In reality, movement in the data may reflect reclassification, re-exporting, or narrow production enclaves rather than systemic learning.

The appearance trap is difficult to detect from within. As long as indicators improve, concerns about depth can be dismissed as overly cautious or pessimistic. It is only when execution falters, costs escalate, or external conditions change that the gap between structure and substance becomes undeniable.

This chapter underscores a central risk: when structure is optimized independently of capability, progress becomes brittle. The system looks advanced but behaves immaturely under stress. Recognizing this trap is a prerequisite for reinterpreting complexity signals with the restraint they require.

The next chapter turns to time—specifically, the mismatch between when complexity signals appear and when capabilities actually mature, and why this temporal misalignment compounds strategic risk.

Chapter 6

Temporal Mismatch: Premature Signals and Lagging Capabilities

Productive capability unfolds over time. It accumulates through repeated cycles of production, failure, adjustment, and learning. These cycles are uneven and often slow, particularly in activities that require coordination across multiple technologies, firms, and institutions. Economic complexity signals, by contrast, can change quickly. This difference in tempo creates a temporal mismatch that is easy to overlook and difficult to correct.

Complexity metrics register the presence of outputs, not the maturity of the processes behind them. A product can appear in export data soon after initial production begins, long before the associated capability has stabilized. Early exports may be supported by external inputs, exceptional effort, or narrowly defined conditions that do not generalize. Yet once the signal appears, it is often interpreted as evidence that the underlying capability has arrived.

This misalignment is especially pronounced in learning-intensive industries. Early success is often followed by long periods of refinement during which costs fall, yields improve, and routines solidify. During this phase, the capability remains fragile. Small disruptions can have outsized effects, and adaptation requires sustained attention. Complexity metrics do not capture this fragility. They flatten time, treating early presence and mature production as equivalent.

The result is premature confidence. When complexity signals are read as milestones rather than as early indicators, decision-makers may assume that subsequent stages of development will follow smoothly. Scaling is planned, integration is deepened, and dependencies are introduced on the assumption that the capability can support them. If the learning curve has not yet been traversed, these moves amplify risk rather than progress.

Temporal mismatch also affects comparative reasoning. Countries at different stages of capability development may display similar complexity profiles, even though the resilience and adaptability of their production systems differ dramatically. Without attention to time, these differences remain hidden, and strategies are built on superficial similarity rather than on developmental reality.

The pressure to move quickly exacerbates the problem. When diversification is treated as urgent, early signals are rewarded disproportionately. The system is encouraged to advance on the basis of appearance, reinforcing the inversion identified in earlier chapters. Capability is expected to “catch up” to structure, rather than structure being allowed to emerge from capability.

This inversion is not merely theoretical. It shapes investment timing, workforce expectations, and institutional commitments. When capabilities lag behind the structures built upon them, correction becomes costly. The system must either absorb inefficiency or retreat from positions it has already claimed.

This chapter highlights time as an overlooked dimension in the interpretation of economic complexity. Signals arrive before capabilities mature, and without restraint, they are read as confirmation rather than as early warnings. Recognizing this temporal mismatch is essential to avoiding escalation based on incomplete learning.

The next chapter examines a related consequence: how apparent complexity can coexist with low resilience, and why diversification on paper does not guarantee robustness in practice.

Chapter 7

Complexity Without Resilience

Resilience is the ability of a production system to absorb shocks, adapt to change, and continue operating under stress. It reflects depth, redundancy, and learning, rather than breadth alone. Economic complexity metrics, however, are largely silent on resilience. They describe the variety and composition of outputs, not the system’s capacity to withstand disruption.

As a result, it is possible—and increasingly common—for economies to exhibit high apparent complexity while remaining structurally fragile. Diversification exists on paper, yet production depends on narrow bottlenecks, external inputs, or tightly coupled arrangements that fail when conditions shift. The export basket appears robust; the underlying system is not.

This fragility is often revealed only under pressure. Supply chain disruptions, demand shocks, technological shifts, or regulatory changes expose weaknesses that were invisible in normal conditions. Firms struggle to substitute inputs, reconfigure processes, or meet quality requirements when circumstances deviate from the narrow band in which production was initially established. The complexity signal remains unchanged, but the capability it was assumed to represent proves insufficient.

One reason for this disconnect is that complexity metrics do not account for redundancy. A product’s presence in exports says little about whether alternative suppliers, processes, or designs exist domestically. Nor does it indicate whether critical inputs can be sourced, repaired, or redesigned locally. Resilience depends on these margins, not on the headline output.

Another reason is that complexity does not capture adaptive capacity. Industries that have learned how to learn—how to diagnose problems, experiment with solutions, and integrate improvements—respond differently to shocks than those that rely on fixed routines or external support. These differences are qualitative and procedural; they do not appear in trade data.

The coexistence of complexity and fragility can persist for extended periods. As long as conditions remain favorable, production continues and exports flow. The illusion of robustness is sustained by stability. When stress arrives, however, the gap between structure and resilience becomes visible, often abruptly and at high cost.

This dynamic has important strategic implications. Decisions based on apparent complexity may embed dependencies that assume resilience where none exists. Integration across sectors, commitments to long-term supply, or expectations of rapid scaling all rely on the system’s ability to cope with variation. When that ability is overestimated, the consequences cascade.

This chapter reinforces a central theme: complexity is not robustness. Diversification does not guarantee resilience, and structural breadth does not substitute for adaptive depth. Without attention to how production systems respond to stress, complexity signals can mislead precisely when reliability matters most.

The next chapter moves from diagnosis to reframing. It asks how economic complexity should be repositioned within strategic reasoning—neither dismissed nor overextended, but treated as what it is: a downstream signal that requires disciplined interpretation.

Chapter 8

Reframing Economic Structure as a Downstream Signal

The preceding chapters have shown how economic complexity metrics drifted from description to inference, and from inference to decision input. Correcting this drift does not require abandoning complexity frameworks. It requires repositioning them—explicitly and deliberately—as downstream signals rather than upstream guides.

Economic complexity should be understood as a reflection of what has already been achieved in production and trade. It describes the structure that emerges after capabilities have been built, routines have stabilized, and learning curves have been partially traversed. Read in this way, complexity is a lagging indicator. It tells a story about past accumulation, not about present readiness or future ease.

This reframing restores proportionality. Complexity metrics can inform understanding of how an economy is expressed in global trade without being asked to speak for the capabilities that produced that expression. They can highlight patterns, reveal correlations, and prompt questions, but they cannot answer those questions on their own.

Treating complexity as downstream also clarifies its appropriate use. As a descriptive signal, it can support retrospective analysis and comparative observation. As an input to strategic decision-making, it must be mediated by additional judgment about capability, learning, and resilience. Without that mediation, the metric invites overreach.

Importantly, this reframing shifts responsibility back to interpretation. The problem is not in the data, but in how meaning is assigned to it. When complexity metrics are positioned as summaries rather than as substitutes, their limitations become features rather than flaws. They signal where further inquiry is warranted, not where confidence is justified.

Reframing also tempers expectations. If complexity is downstream, then increases in complexity cannot be demanded on command. They are outcomes of deeper processes that unfold over time. This perspective resists pressure to accelerate appearance at the expense of substance and discourages the use of complexity scores as performance targets.

This shift has implications for how structure is discussed at the highest levels. Instead of asking what a complexity map allows a country to do next, the more appropriate question becomes what underlying capabilities must exist for the map to change meaningfully. The metric ceases to be a guide and becomes a mirror.

By restoring this order, economic complexity regains its analytical value without distorting strategic judgment. It becomes one signal among many, situated where it belongs—downstream of capability, not in place of it.

The next chapter considers what this reframing implies for strategic interpretation more broadly, without crossing into prescription or policy design.

Chapter 9

Implications for Strategic Interpretation (Without Prescriptions)

Reframing economic complexity as a downstream signal has consequences for how strategic judgment is exercised, even in the absence of policy change. These consequences concern interpretation, sequencing, and confidence—how signals are read, how decisions are paced, and how uncertainty is managed.

The first implication is interpretive restraint. When complexity metrics are no longer treated as proxies for capability, their signals lose their automatic authority. An increase in complexity ceases to be self-validating. It becomes an observation that invites scrutiny rather than endorsement. This restraint does not slow decision-making; it disciplines it.

A second implication concerns sequencing. If complexity reflects accumulated capability rather than latent potential, then it cannot be used to justify leaps ahead of learning. Strategic interpretation must distinguish between what appears feasible in structure and what is executable in practice. This distinction affects timing, scale, and the order in which commitments are made, even when no explicit strategy is articulated.

Third, reframing complexity alters how confidence is formed. Apparent sophistication no longer warrants unqualified optimism. Confidence must be grounded in evidence of learning, resilience, and adaptation—factors that sit outside the metric but determine whether structural advances can be sustained. In this sense, the reframing introduces productive doubt, not paralysis.

These shifts also affect comparative reasoning. Similar complexity profiles across countries or sectors can no longer be assumed to imply comparable capability. Interpretation must account for differences in institutional maturity, firm behavior, and learning history. Without this adjustment, comparison becomes a source of error rather than insight.

Importantly, these implications do not dictate what should be done. They clarify how signals should be read. The paper does not argue for alternative metrics, revised indexes, or new planning tools. It argues for a change in interpretive posture: from inference to inquiry, from appearance to examination.

This posture is particularly relevant in environments where complexity metrics have become embedded in narratives of progress. Reframing does not require abandoning those narratives, but it does require treating them as provisional. Structure is no longer a destination; it is an expression whose meaning depends on what lies beneath it.

By limiting itself to interpretation, this chapter preserves the diagnostic nature of the paper. It avoids prescription while still reshaping the cognitive frame through which economic structure is understood. In doing so, it prepares the ground for more reliable judgment downstream—without crossing into the domain of policy design.

The final chapter closes the diagnostic loop by positioning economic complexity within a broader capability-first system, connecting it conceptually to education upstream and industrial execution downstream.

Chapter 10

Positioning Economic Complexity Within a Capability-First System

Economic complexity does not exist in isolation. It sits within a larger system that links learning, production, and execution over time. When interpreted correctly, it can illuminate how that system expresses itself in trade. When interpreted incorrectly, it can obscure where the system is weak and where attention is most needed.

A capability-first system begins upstream, with how skills are formed, knowledge is accumulated, and learning is sustained. Education and training shape the potential for production, but they do not guarantee it. As established in the Education Paradox, credentials and attainment can rise without translating into productive depth. Capability emerges only when learning is embedded in practice.

Downstream of education lies production itself: firms, routines, supplier networks, and the iterative processes through which competence is refined. This is where capability is tested, reinforced, or eroded. Outcomes appear here as cost trajectories, quality stability, and adaptability under pressure. These outcomes are uneven and difficult to observe from a distance.

Economic complexity occupies a further downstream position. It reflects how the accumulated results of production are expressed in global trade. As such, it is an echo, not a source. It speaks to what has already been consolidated rather than to what is still forming. Positioned in this way, complexity can provide useful confirmation, but it cannot substitute for examination of the layers above it.

When complexity is allowed to stand in for capability, the system becomes inverted. Downstream signals are used to infer upstream strength, and execution is built on assumptions rather than on verified learning. A capability-first framing restores the correct order. Education feeds production, production builds capability, and complexity describes the resulting structure.

This positioning also clarifies the limits of what any single framework can offer. No metric can compress the full richness of industrial capability into a single number or map. Complexity indicators contribute one perspective, valuable within bounds, misleading beyond them. Recognizing this is not a rejection of measurement, but an assertion of proportionality.

The purpose of this paper has been to redraw these boundaries. By clarifying what economic complexity measures, what it omits, and how its signals should be interpreted, it seeks to reduce strategic error before execution begins. It does not propose alternatives or reforms. It insists on diagnosis.

Placed alongside the Education Paradox and followed by a deeper examination of industrial capability and sovereign execution, this reconsideration completes a diagnostic arc. Together, these analyses challenge a common failure mode: mistaking signals for substance. In doing so, they aim to restore discipline to how economic structure is read, and to ensure that decisions are grounded in capability rather than in appearance.
