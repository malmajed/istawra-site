Research, Development and Innovation: Building Capability That Lasts

The purpose of research, development and innovation is larger than the activity it generates. Funding, laboratories, publications, patents and new programmes can all contribute to progress. The strategic question is what they enable people, firms and institutions to do—and whether that ability remains, improves and spreads after an individual project ends.

A national RDI system should therefore be examined through several perspectives together: the quality of knowledge, the ability to use it, the demand for its application, the conditions of investment, and the institutions responsible for carrying learning forward. Strength in one dimension does not remove the need to build the others.

Begin with the capability to be built

A useful starting point is a concrete capability question. Can a firm diagnose a production problem, develop a solution, test it, qualify it and improve it through repeated use? Can a research organisation sustain a technical team, maintain its equipment and carry knowledge into the next project? Can a public institution turn a priority into a sequence of decisions that others can execute?

These questions make ambition more precise. They also clarify what funding must support: people, knowledge, organisations and industrial systems that can perform, learn, absorb technology, improve and innovate. A new facility or a completed research project may contribute to that result, but neither establishes it on its own.

Connect research to firms and productive demand

The private sector contributes more than finance. Firms bring production problems, engineering experience, customers, supply relationships and opportunities to test whether a solution works outside the laboratory. Their participation is most useful when it helps define a problem, shape the development work and provide a credible route to application.

This requires attention to the different roles of established companies, startups, scaleups and innovative SMEs. An established manufacturer may provide an operating environment and sustained demand. A smaller technology company may contribute a specialised solution but need access to testing, qualification, development contracts and patient funding. One participation model will not serve all of them.

Attracting an innovative company is only the beginning. The deeper question is how it becomes embedded in the local ecosystem through engineering work, supplier development, joint research and opportunities for local firms to learn and participate in value creation.

Build the connections that allow knowledge to move

Research organisations and firms work to different time horizons and standards of evidence. A scientific result, an engineering prototype and a qualified product answer different questions. Progress requires deliberate connections between these stages: testing facilities, engineering support, access to equipment, suitable contracting arrangements and people who can work across institutional boundaries.

Foundational research also needs room to pursue questions whose applications are not yet clear. A balanced system protects that work while making the pathway to use more effective where application is the objective. Commercial uptake cannot be the only test of research value, just as scientific output cannot be the only test of industrial readiness.

Match funding to learning and development

Funding should reflect the stage of the work and the uncertainty being reduced. Early investigation, prototype development, testing, qualification and production expansion have different needs. Requiring the same evidence at every stage can exclude promising work or reward activity that does little to resolve the next technical or commercial question.

Milestones can connect funding to meaningful progress, provided they recognise learning as well as delivery. A well-examined result that changes a design or stops an unsuitable route can be valuable. The aim is to improve the quality of the next decision while protecting the teams and knowledge needed for continued development. National alignment informs that judgment; it does not guarantee commercial viability, returns or government support.

Make responsibility clear across the system

A shared ambition does not settle who commissions the work, owns the decision, receives the benefit or delivers the result. These responsibilities need to be explicit. A research team should not carry responsibility for an industrial adoption decision it cannot control; a funder should know which uncertainty its support is intended to resolve; a delivery organisation should know the conditions it must meet.

Coordination becomes practical when information, decisions and unresolved dependencies have a clear route between institutions. Oversight then examines whether the connections are working and whether the evidence supports the next commitment. Counting agreements or meetings alone cannot answer those questions.

Carry capability into the next cycle

The value of a programme extends beyond its closing report. It includes the expertise retained, the methods reused, the equipment kept productive, the supplier relationships strengthened and the ability to address a harder problem next time. Without arrangements that preserve these gains, repeated projects can consume resources while repeatedly rebuilding the same foundations.

The central test of an RDI system is therefore whether successive efforts leave people, firms and institutions more capable. That requires research quality, productive demand, appropriate finance, clear responsibility and sustained learning to reinforce one another. Looking at these perspectives together makes it possible to judge progress by what the system can reliably do—and continue to improve.
